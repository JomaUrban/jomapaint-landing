Laser cutting kerf: what it is and whether you should compensate for it

Kerf in laser cutting is the strip of material the beam melts and blows away. It is small, but it exists, and it affects fits, inside corners and the finest details. The important thing for you: draw to nominal size.

What is kerf in laser cutting?

Kerf, or cut width, is the width of the slot the cut leaves behind. In laser cutting, the beam melts the metal and the assist gas blows it out under the sheet. That material disappears.

If the machine followed your line exactly, the part would come out smaller than drawn, by half a kerf on each side, and the holes larger. That is why the cutting program offsets the path.

A narrow kerf is one of the advantages of laser: it removes little material and, according to Fractory, limits the heat-affected zone. That is why laser allows finer details than plasma or oxy-fuel in thin sheet.

How wide is the kerf of a fibre laser?

There is no single figure. These are the values published by several industry sources:

Laser cut width according to different sources
SourceWhat it saysContext

Durma (machine manufacturer)0.10–0.25 mm in 0.5–3 mm steel or stainless; 0.25–0.50 mm in 4–12 mm carbon steelReference ranges for fibre
SendCutSend0.15–1 mmFibre laser, depending on thickness

These are general industry rules, not measurements of our machines. The sources agree on the order of magnitude (tenths of a millimetre) and that it grows with thickness; they differ at the top end because SendCutSend includes thick plate.

We cut sheet up to 6 mm. In that range, all the sources talk about tenths of a millimetre.

What does the cut width depend on?

  • Thickness. More thickness, more kerf. It is the variable every source mentions.
  • Material and alloy. Each material melts and is blown out differently.
  • Optics and focus. The beam width depends on the focal length of the lens.
  • Power and assist gas. They change the melt pool and how it is expelled.
  • Geometry. SendCutSend points out that the kerf also changes with the shape being cut.

That is why the kerf is set by whoever programs the machine, with their cutting parameters for each material and thickness. The assist gas, for example, also changes the edge: we look at it in nitrogen or oxygen.

For you, the practical consequence is this: there is no number you can subtract in your drawing that works in every case. Durma puts it this way: don't add or subtract a fixed figure on every line.

Do you have to compensate for kerf in your drawing?

No. Draw the part to its nominal size. The cutting program (CAM) offsets the path by roughly half a kerf: outwards on the outer outline and inwards on holes and slots.

If you compensate for it as well, it is compensated twice: the part comes out large and the holes small. Press brake and laser manufacturer Durma sums it up like this: if the cutter already applies the offset on the machine, don't compensate in CAD as well.

In practice If a dimension is critical (a fit, a shaft, a bearing), state it in the notes or on a PDF. We review the file and confirm what we can guarantee on that dimension.

Kerf and parts that have to fit together

In a slot that takes a tab, or in two parts that fit together, the kerf is already compensated. What you have to draw is the clearance you want between them, in nominal dimensions. We explain it in tab and slot design.

Also bear in mind the cutting tolerance, which adds to the clearance. You will find it in laser cutting tolerances.

Common-line cutting is a separate case: two parts sharing the same line. SendCutSend does not accept it and explains that, between such parts, the real gap ends up between 0.15 and 0.30 mm depending on the material. If you design parts that touch, separate them into different files.

Kerf, inside corners and small details

There are no perfectly sharp inside corners: the minimum radius is of the order of the cut width, according to SendCutSend. If another part has to fit into that corner, leave a small relief.

Details narrower than the kerf disappear. In practice, the limit is set not by the kerf but by heat: holes, slots and bridges have larger minimums, which depend on thickness. You will find them in minimum hole, slot and bridge.

Frequently asked questions

How wide is the kerf on your machines?+

We don't publish our own figure: it depends on the material, the thickness and the parameters. We compensate for it in the cutting program.

Should I draw the part larger to compensate for the cut?+

No. Draw the nominal size; the offset is applied by the cutting program.

Does kerf affect the tolerance?+

Kerf compensation is part of the process; the final deviation is governed by the cutting tolerance, not by the kerf.

Is the kerf the same all round the outline?+

Not always. According to SendCutSend, it changes with geometry, assist gas and power, as well as with material. The cutting program takes those variables into account.
